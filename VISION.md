# Product vision

This is the experience TTasks is building toward, not a claim that every part exists today.

## The promise

A calm place to see your work and what is known about it, hand a task to the agent you choose, and return to a list you can trust and a library that has grown.

The product should feel like sitting down at a clear desk. Your work has a place. The next useful action is within reach. You do not have to hold every unfinished thread in your head, or explain the same task from the beginning each time you return to it. What was learned once is there the next time, for you and for every agent that works after you.

The reward is a little more attention for the work itself, and a little less attention spent maintaining the system around it.

## Calm that is earned

Calm comes from clarity. A quiet screen still tells the truth about a busy day. It shows what matters now, what changed while you were away, and what needs your judgement. Important work remains visible even when it is uncomfortable or overdue.

Each fact has a home. Repetition falls away. The actions people use most stay in sight. Details become available where they are useful, without turning every task into a wall of information. The interface should feel settled beneath your hands: an update must not steal your place or interrupt a thought. The ways around the app you use most keep a fixed place and never shift as their contents come and go, so your hand finds them without looking. It answers at once, and the keyboard reaches everything the pointer does. Waiting is a kind of noise.

There is no performance to keep up. A long list is information, not a verdict. Returning after an interruption should feel welcoming. An empty day can simply be empty.

## One space, one tree

A space is one tree of a person's work. Projects sit in the space and in other projects, as deep as the work needs. Tasks sit in the space, in a project or under another task. A project lasts and is put away when its time is over. A task ends.

Every level has a title, a brief and a description. A title is enough to begin. The brief says in one line what the thing is, so a person or an agent can tell from it whether to read further. The description holds what deserves more words.

Knowledge has a place in the same tree. Pages hold what was worked out. Sources hold what came from outside: a mail, a ticket, a document. Notes hold the conversation, in time order. Each can sit at any level, and it sits as high as it is true. What holds for every task of a customer lives on that customer's project, where the next task finds it and where it outlives the task that learned it. The tree follows how the person thinks about the work, not how the folders on disk are arranged.

One space is open at a time, and nothing passes between spaces.

## The relationship with agents

Agents are capable collaborators. They help keep the list current, carry context into the work, record what happened and notice what still needs doing. Their usefulness should be felt in fewer loose ends and fewer repeated explanations.

An agent that takes up a task is handed what is known above it: the title and brief of every level from the space down, and what each level holds. It reads the pages that matter for its work and leaves the rest. As it works, it keeps the library true: it corrects a page that was wrong, adds what the next task will need, and puts it at the level where it belongs. An agent is never made to invent a lesson. When nothing reusable came of the work, it says so.

The person sets the direction and decides what agents may do, through their own agent setup and through what they start. Agents write directly. There is no queue of changes waiting for approval. Confidence comes from seeing every change, understanding it, and taking it back when it was wrong, rather than from supervising every small action in advance.

Activity and achievement are different. Starting work is not finishing it. When something is waiting, unsuccessful or uncertain, the product says so plainly. Quietness must never become reassurance that has not been earned.

## Moments the product is made for

**Arriving.** You open Today and find your bearings: the work you chose to focus on, the shape of the coming days, and the changes that deserve a look. The whole backlog does not compete for attention at once.

**Catching a thought.** Something comes up while you are busy with something else. One field takes it in a few words, and you are back where you were. It can be sorted out later, by you or by an agent.

**Moving into a task.** You open it, add a thought and choose an agent. The task carries its context into the right place: the app you chose, the folder where the work lives, the account it belongs to, and what is known around it. The conversation is yours to continue, in plain sight, and it begins with the work rather than with preparation. This handoff is the defining experience and should have one obvious path.

**Keeping the list true.** As work progresses, the agent records useful developments and captures the follow-up it discovered. Changes are noticeable without becoming a stream of interruptions. You can tell what changed and who changed it.

**Watching the work.** One screen shows, line by line and as it happens, what each agent did and to what.

**Letting work continue.** You ask an agent to work in the background, or set work that comes back on a schedule, and turn your attention elsewhere. When you return, you can understand what ran, what it found, what remains and whether it needs you.

**Finding what is known.** You open a project and see its library: the pages and sources that hold what was learned, and who changed each one and when.

**Recovering.** An agent misunderstood a task, or changed a page for the worse. You see what it did, open the page as it was before, and bring that version back. A check-off, a move to the Trash or a correction of words can be undone at once. Recovering the work in TTasks must never imply that an email already sent or an action taken elsewhere has been undone.

**Finishing and remembering.** Checking a task off feels immediate and quiet. Its outcome stays with it, and what it taught is already in the library above it, so completion clears today's view without erasing the value of the work.

**Away from the desk.** On the phone you see Today, catch a thought, check something off and read a page. The work stays on your computer, and the phone reaches it there. A thought caught while the computer sleeps waits on the phone and arrives once.

**Any kind of work.** A report, a research question, a trip, a form, a project. The experience should make sense across knowledge work and personal tasks, whether an agent is involved or the person simply wants a reliable list.

## Yours to keep

The space holds a person's work, and often what other people wrote to them. That deserves care a person can feel.

The work lives on your own computer, apart from the app, and stays readable without it. The app can be replaced or updated and the work is still there. Nothing is lost by accident: a finished task keeps its outcome, every change keeps its history, and every edit of a text keeps the words it replaced.

Agents act within limits the person sets. Words that arrive from outside, in an email, a ticket or a web page, are something to read, never an order to follow. The same holds for a page an agent wrote: it is knowledge, and only the person's own notes for agents and their skills are instructions. Starting an agent, letting it run by itself and changing what agents may do stay with the person.

A space has one home, and only the home writes. Today the home is your computer, and the phone and other devices reach it there. Later a space may live on a server, so that one person's several computers, or a team, share it. The rule stays the same, and a hosted space can always come home.

## What TTasks is not

It is not a wiki with a task list attached. It opens on Today, and a task is one click from an agent. Pages serve the work.

It does not ask a person to approve what agents write before they may write it. It keeps the way back instead.

It never merges two edited copies of a space. One home writes, so there is never a conflict between copies to resolve.

## Look, mood and character

Warm ink, unbleached paper and a restrained moss accent. Sumi, the dark theme, is the default: deep warm greys with comfortable, legible text. Washi, the light theme, brings the openness of a pale page. Both should feel composed enough to leave open through a working day.

The mood is attentive, grounded and quietly capable. Typography is even and readable. Headings guide rather than announce. Space separates ideas without making useful information feel far away. Colour has a job: it helps someone recognise a project, a state or the next action. Words and shapes carry that meaning too.

Movement acknowledges a change and then settles. The interface respects people who prefer stillness. A calm appearance must remain readable, usable with a keyboard and understandable without relying on colour or animation.

The Zen inspiration is a practice of restraint: remove what does not help, keep what gives the work meaning, leave room for attention. It does not require religious imagery or a collection of Japanese decorative motifs. The TumbleToad identity, and any nature or amphibian touch, should support this feeling lightly, rather than make the workspace busy or childish.

## Voice

Speak like a thoughtful colleague: warm, precise and brief. Name the work and its state. Explain a problem at the moment someone can act on it. Be honest about uncertainty. Avoid guilt, inflated promises and celebrations that demand attention.

The product's confidence should come from dependable behaviour. Its language should help people understand, decide and continue.

## Choosing between good ideas

The task-to-agent handoff comes first, followed by a truthful list and a clear view of today. Knowledge that reaches the next agent by itself comes next. Background work, recoverability and the memory of completed work support that core. Simplicity means reducing effort while preserving understanding. A small core that does these things well is worth more than a wide one.

Before accepting a design, ask:

- Does this help the person understand the next useful action?
- Is each fact shown where it belongs, without needless repetition?
- Does knowledge sit at the level where the next task will find it?
- Can the person reach important actions without hunting?
- Does the person understand what agents may do and what they actually did?
- Are uncertainty and the limits of recovery visible?
- Can people use it comfortably with different ways of seeing, reading and interacting?
- Does it still make sense for research, writing or a personal task, not just code?
- Does the person's work stay theirs: on their machine, readable, and never lost?
- Is TTasks still a task list first, one click from an agent?
- Could it be left out, or folded into something that is already there?
- Does it give attention back to the person, or ask for more of it?

When visual quiet and practical usefulness pull apart, preserve usefulness and find a quieter way to express it.

When speed and a more useful, more complete or more dependable product pull apart, keep the product. A target for speed, memory or size is a guide chosen before anyone had used the thing: real use decides what it should be.
